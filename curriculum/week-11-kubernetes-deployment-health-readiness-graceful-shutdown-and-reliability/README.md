# Week 11 — Kubernetes Deployment, Health/Readiness, Graceful Shutdown & Reliability — Deploying `notes` to a Cluster That Trusts Its Probes and Drains Its Work on `SIGTERM`

Welcome to **C30 · Crunch Go**, Week 11 — the last instructional week before the capstone. Week 10 packaged `notes` into a small, static, non-root, distroless image configured entirely through the environment, and proved it composes with Postgres, Jaeger, Prometheus, and Grafana. This week the image goes to **Kubernetes**. The same image — no rebuild — runs under a Deployment, behind a Service, configured by a ConfigMap and a Secret, answering **liveness and readiness probes that tell the truth**, performing a **graceful shutdown** that drains in-flight work on `SIGTERM` within the termination grace period, and protected by the **reliability patterns** a cloud-native service owes the cluster: timeouts everywhere, retries with exponential backoff and jitter, circuit breaking, and load-shedding under saturation. We do not deploy a new service. We deploy the one we containerized.

The first thing to internalize is that **the readiness probe must tell the truth, and liveness and readiness answer different questions**. Liveness answers "is this process alive, or is it wedged and should be killed?" Readiness answers "should this pod receive traffic right now?" They are different, and conflating them is the single most common cloud-native mistake — the one interviewers use to separate people who have operated a service from people who have not. A liveness probe that checks the database will, when the database hiccups, kill every healthy pod in a restart storm and turn a transient blip into an outage. A readiness probe that *doesn't* check the database will route traffic to a pod that cannot serve it. The rule: **liveness depends on nothing external; readiness depends on everything the pod needs to serve.** The reference is the Kubernetes liveness/readiness/startup-probes doc at <https://kubernetes.io/docs/tasks/configure-pod-container/configure-liveness-readiness-startup-probes/>.

The second thing to internalize is that **graceful shutdown is `context` cancellation applied to the whole process — and it is the disposability factor Week 10 deferred**. When Kubernetes terminates a pod (a rolling deploy, a scale-down, a node drain), it sends `SIGTERM`, removes the pod from the Service endpoints, and waits up to `terminationGracePeriodSeconds` before sending `SIGKILL`. A well-behaved service uses that window: stop accepting new connections, let in-flight requests finish, close the database pool and the gRPC server, flush the trace exporter, and exit cleanly — all before the grace period runs out. This is exactly the `context` cancellation and `errgroup` machinery from Week 4, applied to `main` instead of a worker pool. A service that ignores `SIGTERM` gets `SIGKILL`ed mid-request and drops it on the floor. The reference is the Kubernetes pod-lifecycle/termination doc at <https://kubernetes.io/docs/concepts/workloads/pods/pod-lifecycle/#pod-termination> and Go's `net/http` `Server.Shutdown` at <https://pkg.go.dev/net/http#Server.Shutdown>.

The third thing to internalize is that **a rolling deploy is a handoff, not a swap, and readiness + graceful drain are the two halves that make it drop zero requests**. Kubernetes brings up new pods, waits for *their* readiness probes to pass before adding them to the Service, then terminates the old pods — which drain gracefully because they got `SIGTERM` and were pulled from the endpoints first. At no instant is there no ready pod taking traffic. `maxSurge` and `maxUnavailable` tune how aggressively the rollout proceeds; honest readiness and graceful drain are what make it safe. Remove either — a lying readiness probe or a process that ignores `SIGTERM` — and the rollout drops requests. The reference is the Kubernetes rolling-update / deployment-strategy doc at <https://kubernetes.io/docs/concepts/workloads/controllers/deployment/#rolling-update-deployment>.

The fourth thing to internalize is that **timeouts everywhere is the first reliability pattern, and a service with no timeout is a service waiting to wedge**. Every outbound call — to Postgres, to the OTLP collector, to any downstream — must have a `context` deadline. A call with no timeout, against a slow or dead dependency, holds a goroutine and a connection forever; enough of them and the service exhausts its pool and wedges, and now your *healthy* service is unhealthy because a *dependency* was slow. The `context` you have threaded since Week 4 is the timeout backbone; this week every downstream call gets a deadline. The reference is the Go blog on context at <https://go.dev/blog/context> and the `context` package docs at <https://pkg.go.dev/context>.

The fifth thing to internalize is that **retries need backoff and jitter, or they become a thundering herd that finishes the dependency off**. When a downstream call fails transiently, retrying is right — but retrying immediately, from every client, in lockstep, is how a brief blip becomes a synchronized retry storm that keeps the recovering dependency on its knees. The pattern is exponential backoff (wait longer after each failure) with jitter (randomize the wait so clients do not retry in lockstep), a cap on attempts, and retries *only* on transient, idempotent failures. The reference is the AWS Architecture blog's canonical "Exponential Backoff and Jitter" at <https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/architecture/exponential-backoff-and-jitter/> and the Google SRE book chapter on handling overload at <https://sre.google/sre-book/handling-overload/>.

The sixth thing to internalize is that **a circuit breaker stops you from hammering a dependency that is clearly down, and load-shedding keeps you healthy when you cannot serve everyone**. A circuit breaker watches the failure rate to a dependency; when it crosses a threshold it "opens" and fails fast for a cooldown instead of sending doomed requests, then "half-opens" to test recovery. Load-shedding is the inbound analogue: under saturation, a service that sheds excess load (rejects with 503, bounds its concurrency) stays healthy for the requests it *does* accept, rather than collapsing under all of them — and its readiness probe should reflect that it is shedding. The reference is the SRE book's "Addressing Cascading Failures" at <https://sre.google/sre-book/addressing-cascading-failures/> and the gobreaker library at <https://github.com/sony/gobreaker>.

The seventh thing to internalize is that **`kind` is a real Kubernetes cluster on your laptop, and everything the capstone needs runs on it for free**. `kind` (Kubernetes-in-Docker) runs a genuine cluster in containers; the manifests you write — Deployment, Service, ConfigMap, Secret, the probes — are the same manifests a managed cluster runs. No cloud account, no bill. The reliability drill (the rolling-deploy-under-load and the dependency-outage incident) runs on `kind` exactly as it would on GKE or EKS. The reference is the `kind` quick-start at <https://kind.sigs.k8s.io/docs/user/quick-start/>.

By the end of this week you will be the engineer who can deploy a Go service to Kubernetes with probes that tell the truth, drain its in-flight work on `SIGTERM` instead of dropping it, roll out a new version under load with zero dropped requests, and contain a dependency outage with timeouts, jittered retries, and a circuit breaker — the operational contract a service owes the cluster it runs in, and the contract the capstone defends. That is the difference between someone who writes Go services and someone the platform team trusts in production.

- **Cite** kubernetes.io (probes, deployments, pod lifecycle, ConfigMap/Secret, security), kind.sigs.k8s.io, pkg.go.dev (net/http, context, os/signal, errgroup), the AWS backoff-and-jitter article, and the Google SRE book for each technique covered.

- **`kind` and the cluster.** `kind create cluster`, loading a local image with `kind load docker-image`, the kubeconfig, `kubectl` basics for this week.
- **The core workload objects.** Deployment (replicas, the pod template, the rollout strategy), Service (ClusterIP, the endpoints), ConfigMap (non-secret config), Secret (the database URL), env-from projection.
- **Liveness, readiness, startup probes.** The three probe types; HTTP probes against `/healthz` and `/readyz`; `initialDelaySeconds`, `periodSeconds`, `failureThreshold`; why liveness must not depend on the database and readiness must.
- **Graceful shutdown in Go.** `signal.NotifyContext` for `SIGTERM`; `http.Server.Shutdown` and gRPC `GracefulStop`; the `errgroup` orchestration; closing the pgx pool and flushing the OTel exporter in order; the grace-period budget; the `preStop` sleep and the endpoint-removal race.
- **Rolling updates.** `RollingUpdate` strategy, `maxSurge`, `maxUnavailable`; the readiness gate on new pods; the graceful drain on old pods; why the rollout is a handoff; the load-generator proof of zero drops.
- **Reliability pattern — timeouts.** A `context` deadline on every outbound call; server read/write/idle timeouts; the wedge a missing timeout causes.
- **Reliability pattern — retries with backoff and jitter.** Exponential backoff; full vs equal jitter; the capped attempt count; retrying only transient idempotent failures; the thundering-herd failure mode.
- **Reliability pattern — circuit breaking.** The closed/open/half-open states; the failure-rate threshold; failing fast during cooldown; `gobreaker`.
- **Reliability pattern — load-shedding.** Bounding inbound concurrency (a semaphore / `errgroup.SetLimit`); 503 for excess; reflecting shedding in readiness; the cascading-failure it prevents.

## The "contract a service owes the cluster" promise

C30 has treated `go vet` / `staticcheck` / `go test -race` as the clean-code contract and (Week 10) the distroless-image-from-environment as the container contract. For Week 11 we add the **operational contract**: **the service tells the truth about its readiness, drains its in-flight work on `SIGTERM` instead of dropping it, and contains a dependency's failure instead of amplifying it.** Concretely — a rolling deploy under load drops zero requests because new pods take traffic only after their readiness passes and old pods drain gracefully; a slow dependency does not wedge the service because every call has a timeout; a transient failure is retried with jitter, not a thundering herd; a dead dependency trips a breaker that fails fast instead of piling on; and saturation sheds load rather than collapsing. A service that lies about readiness, ignores `SIGTERM`, or has no timeouts fails this contract — and a hiring manager at a cloud-native shop probes exactly these.

> **Note on tooling.** Cluster: `kind` v0.23+ (Kubernetes 1.30), `kubectl` matching. The same `notes` distroless image from Week 10, loaded with `kind load docker-image`. In-cluster Postgres via a simple Deployment+Service (or the Bitnami/CloudNativePG chart for the stretch). Reliability libraries: the standard library `context` and `errgroup` (`golang.org/x/sync/errgroup`) for timeouts and orchestration; `github.com/sony/gobreaker` for the circuit breaker; the retry-with-jitter is hand-written (it is ~30 lines and you should understand every one). Load generation: `hey` (<https://github.com/rakyll/hey>) or `k6` (<https://k6.io/>). All free and open source; the whole cluster runs on a laptop with no cloud account.
